What history shows

A Who who takes on the How

Again and again, a respected leader reached much further because a trusted partner took on the How. These are stories from the public record. They are not endorsements of SuperWho, and not a claim that these people are affiliated with us. We do not use their photographs or logos here.

Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger

Buffett was already skilled at buying cheap, “cigar-butt” companies, the way Benjamin Graham had taught him. Munger, his longtime partner, kept pressing a different How: buy wonderful businesses at fair prices, because the old method would not scale. Buffett later wrote that it took Charlie to set that course, that Berkshire was built to Charlie’s blueprint, and that Charlie was the architect of the Berkshire we know today.

Source: Warren Buffett, Berkshire Hathaway letters, 2014 and 2023.

Walt Disney and Roy Disney

When Walt Disney won a cartoon contract in 1923, he asked his brother Roy, who was recovering in a veterans’ hospital, to go into business with him. Roy took on the finance and business side so Walt could lead the creative work. After Walt’s death, Roy led the company through the building of Walt Disney World, which opened in 1971 with the company carrying no outstanding debt.

Source: Walt Disney Family Museum; D23, The Official Disney Encyclopedia.

Elon Musk and Gwynne Shotwell

Musk set SpaceX’s technical and strategic direction. In 2008 he asked Gwynne Shotwell, already at the company, to serve as president, focused on day-to-day operations — sales, manufacturing, launch, and finance. As president and chief operating officer she has run those operations and customer relationships; NASA has noted that she built the Falcon launch manifest to more than 100 missions, representing more than $10 billion in business.

Source: SpaceNews, December 2008; NASA biography of Gwynne Shotwell.

Dan Sullivan and Dr. Benjamin Hardy

Sullivan had built Strategic Coach and written for his own clients, but he has said he did not have the skill, or the feel for a general audience, to write a major-market book himself. Hardy became that Who — the writer who could take the Who Not How idea to a wider public. The result was Who Not How, and then further books together, including 10x Is Easier Than 2x.

Source: Dan Sullivan, Strategic Coach; How To Win Big By Doing Less.

Dr. Benjamin Hardy found a Who, too

Hardy has said that even after he became the writer, he got stuck: it took almost a year to get the book deal, and he could not negotiate it or get the writing moving on his own. He found Whos, including Tucker Max, who coached and edited, and Hay House, which published the book. Hardy later said Who Not How was the easiest, most powerful book he had written, because of that collaboration.

Source: Benjamin Hardy on the BiggerPockets podcast; Strategic Coach.

From nothing to something

History also shows people who started small — a salesperson, a cafe owner, a man on the road with a machine to sell — and reached much further because someone took on a piece of the How. These, too, are stories from the public record, not endorsements or affiliations.

Sara Blakely and the mill owner who said yes

Sara Blakely was selling fax machines door to door, with $5,000 in savings and an idea she had after cutting the feet off a pair of pantyhose. Mill after mill turned her down, until Samuel Kaplan, co-owner of Highland Mills in Charlotte, called back two weeks later: he had run the idea by his two daughters, and they told him to help. They spent a year making a prototype; Neiman Marcus put Spanx on the floor, it sold out, and the company grew from a spare room into a brand known around the country.

Source: Sara Blakely, Inc.; Forbes, April 2, 2001.

Harland Sanders and Pete Harman

Harland Sanders ran a small cafe in Corbin, Kentucky, when he visited Leon “Pete” Harman, who owned a restaurant in Salt Lake City. In 1952, Harman became Sanders’s first franchise partner and put the fried chicken on the menu at Harman Café. When Sanders came back through town two weeks later, cars were lined up down the street, and that success led him to franchise the recipe far more widely; Kentucky Fried Chicken grew into a national chain.

Source: Deseret News, January 20, 2012; HISTORY.

Ray Kroc and Harry Sonneborn

In 1954, Ray Kroc was a 52-year-old milkshake-machine salesman when he became the franchise agent for the McDonald brothers’ San Bernardino hamburger stand. The young company was short of money as it tried to grow, until Harry Sonneborn, a finance man Kroc hired, proposed that McDonald’s lease or buy the restaurant sites and rent them to franchisees. Kroc later called that plan “the beginning of real income for McDonald’s,” and the chain grew into a national system.

Source: McDonald’s corporate history; Daniel Gross, “Ray Kroc, McDonald’s, and the Fast-Food Industry,” in Forbes Greatest Business Stories of All Time (Wiley).
